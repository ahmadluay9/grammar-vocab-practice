#### Positive features of a city
> World-class cities have many attractive features you'll want to include in your writing.

1. Paris has **magnificent architecture**.
2. San Francisco has **stunning views** of the bay and hills.
3. Singapore has so much **cultural diversity**.
4. Rome has amazing **ancient monuments**.
5. Shanghai has a **vibrant art scene**.

#### Negative features of a city
> Unfortunately, large urban areas often have serious social and economic problems.

1. **Unemployment** is really high in Chicago.
2. Tampa has a terrible problem with *homelessness*.
3. There is a lot of **crime** in most huge cities.
4. I hate seeing **trash** on the streets of my city.

### Grammar - Reordering sentences for emphasis
#### Reordering sentences for emphasis
> Sometimes speakers and writers want to focus attention on a particular thing or idea. One way to do that is to change the order of a sentence. In some grammar books, this is referred to as a **cleft sentence**. Here are some examples:

**Starting a sentence with 'what'**

Compare the two sentences. Notice in the second sentence the heavy stress on the word **love**, and it begins with the word **what**

1. I love looking at modern architecture.
2. **What** I **love** is looking at modern architecture.

> Despite their complexity, these sentences are quite common in English. They are a good way to emphasize something particular in a sentence. Look at the next pairs of regular and reordered sentences and compare the stress levels.

1. It is interesting how the city has changed.
2. What is interesting is how the city has changed.
3. I like the efficient subways in Tokyo.
4. What I like about Tokyo is the efficient subways.
5. I'm interested in shopping.
6. What I'm interested in is shopping.

### Writing - Writing about a city
#### Describing a city
> One way to write an interesting city description is to use a variety of descriptive words to communicate your emotions. Be careful with the **-ing** and **-ed** word endings. Words that end in **-ing** often describe a feature of something, whereas words that end in **-ed** describe feelings.

1. The homelessness in Tampa was **shocking**.
2. I was **shocked** by the homelessness in Tampa.

> Here are a few more pairs of descriptive words.

1. disgusted – disgusting
2. amazed – amazing
3. disturbed – disturbing
4. confused – confusing
5. surprised – surprising

##### Softening negative remarks

> You never know who will read your blog post, so if you are going to say something negative about someone's city, you should be **diplomatic**. One way is to preface it with a clause that softens your opinion.

1. **I hate to say it**, but homelessness is a problem there.
2. **I don't want to offend anyone**, but crime was a problem there.
3. **I'm sorry to say this**, but it was disgusting.

Another way to soften a negative opinion is to use qualifiers like **a little** or **a bit**.

1. It was **a little** disgusting.
2. She was **a bit** disturbed.
